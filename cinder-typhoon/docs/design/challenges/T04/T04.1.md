## Challenge description — player-facing

Read the supplied training-tank observation and recover how much water it actually contains, distinguishing the measurement from the requested level.

## Story purpose

Establishes the difference between intention and observation.

## Prerequisites and starting position

**Required access or evidence:** the supplied training workspace.

**Starting material:** Small training snapshots, readable field and status legends, a private practice tank, and a separate reporting replay case.

**Campaign references:** No separately named capability output.

## Completion and downstream use

Recover the measured volume bound to its sample and units. A target setting or unitless guess is insufficient.

## Difficulty and inspiration

One small record with readable field meanings.

**Inspiration:** Original challenge for this campaign.

## Technical design

### Surface and normal behavior

The training HMI displays a requested level alongside a timestamped tank
observation from a deterministic practice model. The observation includes tag,
sample ID, engineering units, value, and quality. The immutable VC-017 record is
served at `/captures/volume.json`: sample VS-017 measures 1,200 L with good
quality while its requested volume is 1,000 L. `/field-guide.md` defines the
fields. This capture is not the live tank.

### Vulnerability and intended solution

This is a measurement-literacy task. The intended solution is to distinguish a
setpoint from the independent observation and bind the reported volume to its
sample and units, using the supplied HMI and tag legend.

### Evidence and completion

The capture binds its measured volume to sample ID, engineering units,
quality, and timestamp. The requested level is a separate field and is not
substituted for the measurement.

### Boundaries

The capture is immutable and separate from the writable practice tank.
Practice requests cannot alter this recorded sample.

### Author checks

Check that requested and measured quantities differ, units and quality are
visible, the recorded sample is unique, and live practice cannot alter it.
