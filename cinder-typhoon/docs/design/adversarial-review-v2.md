# Adversarial review of challenge architecture draft 2

Review date: September 18, 2026. Reviewed the preserved draft-2
[architecture](challenge-architecture-v2.md), [64-operation portfolio](operation-portfolio-v2.md),
[allocation ledger](portfolio-v2.csv), [entry routes](entry-routes-v2.json),
[reference catalog](reference-catalog-v2.md), [threat inspiration](threat-inspiration-v2.md),
and [calibration plan](calibration-v2.md). Compared the revision with the
[first review](adversarial-review.md) and Polaris's mission progression.

This assesses the design that exists today. It does not assume the proposed
revisions below have been made. Participant-reception judgments are qualitative
inferences from that design, conditional on competent delivery. They are not
survey results, completion forecasts, or estimates of a review score. Missing
implementation is expected at this stage and is not itself an adverse finding.

## Verdict

Draft 2 is substantially better. It now supports a credible, interesting event
with a reasonable prospect of strong participant reviews. Its clearest strengths
are accessible earned access to the customer, the supplier-to-customer transition,
renewed easy wins inside ARWC, and difficult optional work available early.
The agent belongs in the design as a capability that expands participation.

My present expectation is **a well-received event with several excellent moments**.
Confidence is moderate. The architecture does not yet support expecting equally
strong enthusiasm across its unusually broad audience, or a consistently
memorable sixteen-hour experience. Its largest remaining weakness is the
connection between its substantial technical depth and its campaign identity.

The current portfolio fits competitive exploitation specialists more clearly
than it fits every kind of elite operator. Several demanding investigations
have weakly specified campaign consequences. A participant could appreciate
their technical quality while finding the surrounding operation less compelling.
That is an addressable design problem, not an argument against known CTF sources.

| Review question | Current judgment |
| --- | --- |
| Is it interesting? | Yes. Actual customer consumption, conflicting operational records, and the distinction between seeing and controlling the plant supply good questions. Some optional work still has a stronger mechanism than purpose. |
| Does it align with professional design guidance? | Broadly yes: progressive access, partial credit, meaningful alternatives, accessible beginnings, source-aware calibration, and independent effect verification. The alignment does not validate the precise counts or predict enjoyment. |
| Does it show taste? | More clearly. FieldLink, competent recurring people, bounded consequences, and restrained AI content fit together. The repeated use of legacy diagnostics and recovered artifacts needs tighter selection. |
| Does it tie to the story? | The principal campaign does. The connection is uneven across the optional investigations that must sustain much of the event. |
| Is the distribution defensible? | The overall tier mix is defensible. The concentration of advanced work in particular specialties deserves revision. Counts alone cannot establish sixteen hours of worthwhile play. |
| Will it be fun? | There is a credible basis for discovery, competence, experimentation, and choice. The moment-to-moment co-hacking experience and the motivation to continue optional work remain less concrete. |
| Will it be memorable? | The customer transition and actual gate effect are strong candidates. More players need comparably satisfying local conclusions, including those who never finish the campaign. |

## Professional comparison and limits

Rechecked the following primary sources for this review. Their useful principles
are not universal requirements, and their published formats differ from this event.

| Source | Relevant comparison |
| --- | --- |
| [SEI challenge-development guidance](https://www.sei.cmu.edu/documents/1299/2022_005_001_888602.pdf) | Sections 2.2 and 3 connect challenges to applicable skills, audience difficulty, multipart progress, and effort-sensitive scoring. Draft 2 follows these principles. The remaining question is whether its hardest work represents enough of this audience's operational strengths. |
| [PPP organizer guidance](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pwning/docs/master/suggestions-for-running-a-ctf.markdown) | Useful simultaneous problems and worthwhile technical ideas matter. Draft 2's three-lead target follows that intent. PPP's global unlock recommendation belongs to its competition model; this campaign can preserve individual earned access while offering choices at each earned position. |
| [rCTF challenge-design guidance](https://rctf.osec.io/meta/running-a-successful-ctf/#challenge-design) | Its ordinary jeopardy guidance favors a range within categories and clear, worthwhile problems. Draft 2's broad middle is compatible; its subject-specific ceiling is less balanced. Its numerical recipe is not a mandate for this event. The user's explicit acceptance of known challenge inspiration also supersedes its preference for original problems. |
| [SANS Holiday Hack 2025](https://www.sans.org/cyber-ranges/holiday-hack-challenge) | The announced combination of short confidence-building tasks and larger story capstones supports the same general rhythm. Cinder has approachable tasks and a final climax; optional campaign capstones deserve comparable attention. Holiday Hack's long availability and challenge skipping are different choices. |
| [Mechanics, Dynamics, Aesthetics](https://www.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/MDA.pdf) | This design lens distinguishes challenge, discovery, narrative, expression, and fellowship. Cinder now serves challenge and discovery more concretely than personal expression or a shared event experience. It supplies a useful critique vocabulary, not a predictive formula for fun. |
| [Human–AI collaboration study, 2026 preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20446v1) | Its 41 surveyed participants, predominantly students, reported both useful assistance and frustrations with actionability and incorrect suggestions. AI familiarity also mattered. This supports teaching verification and recovery alongside delegation; it cannot predict this provided agent's performance or reactions from elite professionals. |

The design is professionally defensible. None of these sources establishes an
ideal 240-challenge allocation, an AI speed multiplier, or the probability of a
strong review. The reception assessment below is an editorial judgment.

## Distribution audit

Recomputed from the CSV and ran the document validator. The allocation and
declared easy/medium entry closures pass. The validator does not establish
runtime reachability, difficulty, source reproduction, or enjoyment.

| Tier | Slots | Share |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Easy | 63 | 26.3% |
| Medium | 94 | 39.2% |
| Hard | 48 | 20.0% |
| Expert | 24 | 10.0% |
| Elite | 11 | 4.6% |

Shares are independently rounded. Easy/medium work is 157 of 240 slots, or
65.4%. It is 70.2% of KeplerOps and 56.7% of ARWC. Both organizations span all
five tiers, and ARWC's approachable work extends beyond its arrival chapter.
That is a sensible response to the audience and the requirement for progression.

The more revealing distribution is by principal activity:

- Native, application, identity, and crypto contain **74 of 83 hard-or-higher
  slots**, or 89.2%.
- Supply, cloud, and process contain **66 slots, all easy or medium**. These
  counts include the corresponding training allocations.
- The eleven elite milestones occupy seven operations based on six distinct
  precedents. K19 and K20 divide the same University chain.
- Each of the fifteen non-training chapters contains exactly four operations.

Category labels apply to whole operations, so they underdescribe mixed work.
Hard identity and application challenges can be central to a supply campaign;
W28 makes control acquisition hard even though W30 is conditionally medium.
These counts therefore do not prove that advanced supply or OT work is absent.
They do show where the explicitly difficult thinking is concentrated.

There is no reason to force AI into harder tiers or make every category equally
large. The issue is audience fit and variety of demanding work, not symmetry.

## Findings

### R1: The upper tier serves CTF specialties more clearly than operational breadth

**Priority: high. Evidence: strong for the allocation; audience reaction is an inference.**
References: portfolio activity table; K04, K12–K20, W16, W23, W27, and W28.

The native, browser, identity, and cryptographic precedents are legitimate
technical depth. However, the cloud chapter ends at medium, and the difficult
OT-adjacent work largely concerns binaries, timing, or a legacy checksum.
The main process questions remain approachable. This makes the hardest work
legible to a competitive exploitation specialist without establishing equally
demanding campaign planning, cloud reasoning, or process-informed operations.

Elite operational experience and elite CTF specialization overlap without being
interchangeable. An experienced operator may enjoy the existing portfolio and
still find that the promised campaign becomes easier just where their own
professional judgment becomes most useful. Conversely, a specialist can find
excellent exploitation without needing much understanding of the campaign.

**Revision:** give a small number of optional capstones substantial operational
joins across existing work. For example, retaining a precisely scoped supplier
capability across a release change could require combining identity, delivery,
and workload evidence. An ARWC capstone could make a difficult component exploit
useful only after reconciling its effect with current process evidence. Retain
named CTF mechanisms and use MITRE sequences to supply their purpose. Specify
actual decisions and constraints, rather than increasing difficulty labels.
Keep both ordinary customer-entry routes medium and preserve the accessible
process introduction.

### R2: Several demanding side operations lack a satisfying campaign consequence

**Priority: high. Evidence: strong about the briefs; enjoyment is untested.**
References: architecture section 7; portfolio K04, W16, W23, W27, and W32.

The customer acceptance receipt has excellent causality: the player can see
their supplier action arrive at another organization. The return to the same
ticket from the customer's side is a specific, economical callback.

The optional upper tier is less consistent. W16 recovers calibration data;
W23 changes an estimator; W27 obtains acceptance for a changed diagnostic
program; W32 explains replay disagreement. These are technical effects, but
their value to Cinder's operation is not equally clear. What becomes possible,
what belief changes, or what completed objective does the handler acknowledge?

Not every side challenge needs to unlock another service. A self-contained
technical achievement can be satisfying. But a long story-driven event should
give its major optional investigations conclusions with comparable care to
the main transition. Otherwise players spending hours on them experience a
different, less connected event.

**Revision:** attach a concrete local objective and observable conclusion to
each substantial investigation. Define the use of the recovered data, the
operational meaning of the false estimate, or the bounded effect of the accepted
program. Keep the final independent process evaluator trustworthy. A successful
optional compromise must not amount to falsifying the scoreboard's own truth.
Use the recurring people and ticket to make consequences recognizable. Further
character names and dialogue can wait; causal payoffs cannot all wait.

### R3: The content budget still risks counting too many stages of too few ideas

**Priority: medium. Evidence: allocation patterns and specific candidates, not proof of filler.**
References: portfolio K02, K06, K07, K19/K20, and W30; catalog C05/C10.

The anti-padding rule is correct and already acknowledges A01/A02 and W30's
opening/verification pair. It has not yet been applied across the portfolio.
K02 lists understanding, recovering, tracing, altering, and observing a test
job; five verbs do not establish five substantive challenges. K07's model,
exploit, and protected action could be one connected achievement with useful
partial credit, depending on their actual work.

Similarly, C05 supplies thirteen allocated slots across three operations, and
University supplies eight across K19/K20. Reusing different portions is
defensible, but those counts do not multiply the original challenge's difficult
insights or preserve its whole-chain rating for each part. Eleven elite
milestones should not be read as eleven independent long investigations.

The exact four-operation pattern in every campaign chapter is an editorial
warning sign. It may be a harmless planning grid. It should not determine the
final shape of a chapter whose natural rhythm is different.

**Revision:** apply the existing deletion test before protecting the totals.
Distinguish an independent insight, a meaningful exploit milestone, and a
confirmation of the same result. Budget effort and points at operation level.
Replace weak allocations with different worthwhile content and allow uneven
chapter lengths. Preserve the sixteen-hour ambition; this finding does not
justify reducing the event to a short board.

### R4: The alternative process-interpretation route promises evidence it does not supply

**Priority: high. Evidence: direct inconsistency.**
References: architecture section 6; portfolio W09, W18, W19, W21, and W29/W30.

The capability table allows W19 to replace the combination of W18 tag mapping,
W09 allocation data, and W21 project revision. Its declared output identifies
the gate group, valid operating envelope, and economic consequence.

W19's actual brief recovers hidden diagnostic logic and an alternative process
mapping. That does not establish current allocation data, current deployment
revision, or all the operating-envelope evidence. The substitution is broader
than its stated output. W29 also supplies the release-volume reasoning but is
not named in the finale's prerequisite expression; the document needs to say
where that understanding comes from on each valid route.

**Revision:** define the evidence contract for process interpretation. W19 can
replace the mapping component while retaining the necessary data/revision
joins, or its authored outputs can explicitly supply equivalent evidence.
Resolve whether W29 is necessary knowledge, an optional explanation, or an
alternative source of that knowledge. Do this without adding an arbitrary final
exam. The player must have enough information to understand the achieved effect.
This is a dependency decision and does not require topology.

### R5: Broad availability needs a clearer reason to choose, continue, and return

**Priority: high for experience design. Evidence: missing choice contracts, not measured pacing failure.**
References: architecture sections 5, 7, and 9; portfolio availability conditions.

Two seven-achievement customer-entry routes are a strength. They should not be
lengthened merely to consume more time. However, most of the event's intended
breadth lies outside those routes and the final control path. Its value depends
on participants wanting to pursue that optional work.

The three-useful-lead rule and day-two evidence summary are good starting points.
They do not yet show how a player compares the choices, how an abandoned
investigation offers a useful stopping point, or why someone who reaches the
gates early returns to unfinished KeplerOps work. Points can motivate some
players; local objectives and curiosity must carry more of the audience.

The right question is not whether seven medium achievements are too short.
There is no solve-time evidence for that claim. It is whether the player's next
hour has an intelligible purpose beyond increasing their flag count.

**Revision:** sketch the visible choices at the initial desk, corporate ARWC,
read-only OT access, and after the reservoir objective. For each choice, state
the question being investigated, broad kind of work, expected scope, and payoff
without revealing its solution. Author a few optional objectives that remain
valuable after campaign completion and satisfying conclusions for people who
never get there. Day two should recover an intention as well as credentials.
Avoid global unlock schedules and compulsory completion of the optional work.

### R6: The co-hacking promise needs one concrete example of enjoyable play

**Priority: medium. Evidence: an underspecified experience, not a claim that agents reduce fun.**
References: architecture sections 4 and 7; calibration sections 2 and 5.

The design correctly allows deep delegation and makes evidence and effects
visible. That supports the intended uplift. It still describes the interface
more clearly than the participant's experience of choosing an objective,
watching an attempt, recognizing uncertainty, and redirecting the work.

A novice can obtain flags while understanding little of what changed. An
experienced participant can spend time reading an agent's repeated attempts
without useful control over direction. Those are plausible failure modes, not
inevitable properties of assistance. The current calibration questions would
detect them, but a worked design example could prevent some of them.

**Revision:** storyboard one short episode containing the player's objective,
the agent's proposed investigation, an understandable observation, a decision
to continue or change approach, and the visible result. Include recovery from
a mistaken assumption. Teach these habits in training alongside the technical
tasks. Do not force command typing, human approval of every step, or artificial
restrictions on autonomous solving.

### R7: The individual campaign has little specified shared event experience

**Priority: medium; an opportunity for stronger reviews, not a core correctness defect.**
References: architecture sections 7 and 9.

Individual progress and isolation are appropriate. They do not decide whether
300 people feel part of an event. The current experience can be read as many
parallel solo campaigns, with a scoreboard supplying most of the common context.
The host conference may provide social energy, but the scenario does little
yet to create its own shared memories.

**Revision:** choose a light shared layer that preserves individual scoring:
optional short handler briefings, non-spoiling recognition of varied milestone
types, and an end-of-day account of what the fictional operation achieved.
Clarify the boundary between discussing methods and sharing live answers so
novices can seek help without guessing the rules. No shared access gates,
mandatory teams, pauses in play, or dependence on another participant is needed.

This is a design inference about conference appeal. The cited design guidance
does not establish that these additions will increase review scores.

## What is not an adverse finding at this stage

The absence of topology, SDL, finished dialogue, measured solve times, and a
300-player load test is expected. The architecture explicitly reserves that
work. Its prototype plan addresses the main technical uncertainties, including
browser behavior, timing noise, agent assistance, and the process model.

Known public solutions are permitted and useful. Their existence does not
invalidate a challenge. Likewise, keeping AI targets easy/medium, making the
ending attainable without elite completion, and allowing some participants to
leave much of the portfolio unfinished are appropriate choices.

The review does not require equal difficulty distributions in every subject,
equal time in each organization, a guaranteed finish, or a catastrophic story.
The revisions above improve the intended experience within those constraints.

## Likelihood of strong participant reviews

Here, a strong review means that the participant felt capable of meaningful
progress, found worthwhile challenges, understood their accomplishments, and
would recommend the experience. It does not mean they finished the campaign
or ranked highly.

On that basis, **strong reviews are a credible and reasonably likely outcome
for parts of the audience; broadly positive reception is my overall expectation**.
The present architecture supports moderate confidence in that assessment.
Consistently enthusiastic recommendations across the full capability range are
less well supported. The relative sizes of the audience groups are unknown,
so there is no defensible weighted event rating or numerical probability.

| Participant perspective | Current reception outlook | Main reason it could fall short |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First-time hacker using the provided agent | Promising, with substantial upside from early successes, optional training, and attainable customer access. | Flags without an intelligible sense of achievement; too many unexplained choices after the introduction. |
| Intermediate practitioner or experienced generalist | Strongest design fit and the clearest basis for enthusiastic reviews. Familiar workflows, new specialties, real progression, and agent uplift reinforce one another. | Repetition or optional operations that feel disconnected from the mission. |
| Strong competitive exploitation/reversing specialist | Promising. Early access to named, demanding mechanisms respects their time and offers substantial technical interest. | Adaptations lose the source constraints, source-aware agents collapse the work, or long investigations end without a satisfying payoff. |
| Elite enterprise/cloud operator | Mixed-to-promising. Identity chains and the supplier/customer relationship are relevant, but difficult operational synthesis is less explicit. | Cloud-specific work ends at medium; the harder identity work has stronger representation than demanding campaign decisions across domains. |
| Experienced OT/controls practitioner | The bounded process fiction and separation of observation, authority, and effect are credible attractions. Confidence in exceptional reviews is lower. | Process judgment stays shallow, and advanced work feels like unrelated software puzzles placed near engineering artifacts. |

These are overlapping perspectives, not fixed player tracks or assumptions
about an employer's personnel. An elite operator can also be an excellent CTF
specialist; a newcomer can become effective quickly with the agent.

Three design choices most support positive reception: a participant can cause
a meaningful effect early; their changed package can visibly become a customer
foothold; and later access opens new approachable work instead of raising every
task to expert. These are strong foundations for satisfaction without universal
completion.

Three choices most limit the forecast: optional depth often has an incomplete
story conclusion; the advanced portfolio favors particular specialties; and
the design has not yet made sixteen hours of choosing and delegating feel as
coherent as its main campaign route. Those concerns remain even under competent
technical delivery, which is why they belong in this architecture review.

The next revision should first fix R4's evidence substitution, then strengthen
the connection between advanced work and campaign outcomes in R1/R2. Applying
the distinct-work audit and writing the choice/agent episodes in R3/R5/R6 would
make the content budget and player experience more convincing. R7 offers a
smaller addition with potential conference value. None requires selecting the
network topology or promising that everyone reaches the gates.
