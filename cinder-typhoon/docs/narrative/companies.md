# The two companies

These are authored business and social facts. Headcounts describe the size of
the employers; they do not prescribe accounts, inboxes, or machines. The local
setting and everyday stories are developed in [workplace life](workplace-life.md).

## KeplerOps Software

Canonical domain: **keplerops.com**. Founded in 2016; 64 staff in September 2026.

### What it does and why customers pay for it

KeplerOps began as an integration consultancy. Leah Calder-Voss repeatedly saw
field jobs that were physically complete but remained administratively open.
Photographs, spreadsheets, contractor submissions, and revised drawings existed
in different places. Assembling a package a customer could accept meant asking
busy people for the same things again.

The company turned that recurring work into **FieldKest**, a product connecting
field submissions, maintenance packages, asset records, and operational reports.
It preserves relationships between records and delivers them into customers'
established business processes. Customers buy less repeated administration,
support for their particular interfaces, and a supplier who will help when an
exchange stops making sense. FieldKest does not operate plant equipment.

KeplerOps serves roughly a dozen utility organizations. A customer contract can
cover several departments and sites. Recurring enterprise subscriptions and
support provide the majority of annual revenue; scoped onboarding, migrations,
and integration projects provide the rest. These are substantial organizational
contracts, with implementation often spanning months. The business is larger
than a dozen inexpensive software subscriptions would sustain.

KeplerOps is privately held and founder-controlled, with minority holdings among
some early employees and no institutional growth investor. It is profitable,
maintains a working cash reserve, and normally funds hiring from retained
earnings and contracted work. The commercial team spends considerable time on
renewals, procurement exercises, and expansion within existing customers.
ARWC is a valuable reference and renewal; other contracts sustain the business
through the uneven timing of individual projects.

This arrangement has a cost employees can recognize. Promising another bespoke
integration may finance useful hiring while consuming the same engineers who
were going to simplify the product. Declining the work protects capacity but
can make a careful growth plan feel frustratingly slow.

Functional managers report into Leah's leadership group. Rowan works through
product engineering; Maya and Talia are peers in support and implementation,
with different responsibilities. Evan and Noor sit within platform and release.
Talia's support quality role is distinct from the product quality function.

Employees value recognizable customers, room to develop expertise, and a company
large enough to offer help without making every decision remote. The drawbacks
are limited management vacancies and the tendency for an experienced person's
old responsibilities to follow them after their role changes. Specialist
progression exists alongside management, but employees are still learning how
to make a case for it. Rowan wants to stay technical. Maya is considering team
leadership. Neither choice is treated as the more mature ambition.

### Culture in practice

The strongest form of praise is having another colleague use something you
made and tell you how it helped. Short demonstrations often include a repaired
irritation rather than a new feature. Maya has made a habit of crediting the
support colleague who recognized a pattern before the engineer reproduced it.

Conversation is informal, but the work has several local cultures. Release
staff like a published agenda and a definite ending. Developers can become
animated over an example and continue the discussion after everyone else has
left. Support prefers a short conversation followed by a usable written account.
Business operations wants decisions early enough to arrange the work around them.

Most locally based staff use Wednesday and Thursday as common office days;
individual arrangements vary. Remote colleagues participate in demonstrations
and decisions, and the written follow-up matters because the office conversation
does not reach everyone. An occasional failure to include enough context is
corrected as a working habit, rather than celebrated as evidence of a fast culture.

New staff receive a buddy from their own function and introductions elsewhere.
The unofficial sign of belonging is being asked for an opinion on a real piece
of work. Talia remembers how long she spent hearing explanations before anyone
asked what she thought, and tries to shorten that interval for others.

Leah likes people bringing her a question. She also wants managers to make more
decisions themselves. Her tendency to offer one more suggestion after a decision
has been delegated produces mild frustration, including for Leah when she notices
she has done it again. She can take a reminder without turning it into a dispute.

There is no universal appetite for social events. Some people lunch together;
others leave for errands or prefer a quiet break. The office's small traditions
survive because somebody enjoys keeping them going. They are not attendance tests.

### What it wants next

Leah intends to add a few suitable customers over the next two years while
reducing the proportion of work that requires customer-specific engineering.
She wants a durable specialist company where someone can build a long career.
Commercial staff would like a more repeatable demonstration and clearer answers
about which requested integrations the company will take on.

Staff disagree about pace. Rowan would spend more time simplifying old product
behaviour. Maya thinks some improvements become obvious only when support is
given time to work with customers. Noor wants maintenance work to compete fairly
with visible product work. These disagreements continue alongside a useful
release, an ordinary Rillhaven workshop, a new-starter cohort, and plans for
the company's tenth-anniversary gathering. People can enjoy their jobs while
wanting the company to work differently.

## Alterra Regional Water Company

Canonical domain: **alterrawaterco.com**. Formed in 2009; about 230 staff serving
roughly 160,000 residents and local businesses in September 2026.

### Ownership and public purpose

ARWC is jointly owned by the fictional municipal districts of **Pine** and
**River**. Together they form its service area. It supplies drinking water from
source through treatment and distribution, including household billing and
meter services. Wastewater remains with separate municipal services. Municipal
facilities are water customers alongside households and commercial premises.

In this authored governance model, the owners appoint a joint board with district
representatives and independent members. The board approves the service plan,
budget, and rate proposals through the setting's public approval process.
A general manager leads the company. Luc Venn-Morel, director of regional
operations, reports to that general manager and is responsible for operational
planning, maintenance coordination, and presenting the service implications of
major decisions. He does not personally govern every department.

Pine and River remain service and cost centres inside one company. ARWC keeps
district budgets and allocation responsibilities so it can explain expenditure
and commitments to each owner. Shared regional costs follow the agreed allocation
method; an expense belongs to a particular district when that district receives
the specified service. Ownership does not imply equal water use or an automatic
equal split of every bill.

Water charges support operations and planned renewal. Longer-lived projects
have separately approved capital funding. Public board reporting, annual service
results, customer explanations, and consultation over planned work are ordinary
parts of the company's calendar. This is a fictional institutional arrangement,
not an assertion about any particular province's water law.

### Water and the people who use it

Most customers expect dependable water, an understandable bill, and reasonable
notice of a meter visit or planned work. Commercial users have different demand
patterns. Belvarn's packaging plant plans production ahead; Talvern's growers
include nurseries and producers of high-value horticultural crops. They have
separately contracted supplementary seasonal irrigation allowances drawn from
ARWC's treated distribution supply. Those allowances are a limited, discretionary
use, distinct from household service and from any growers' other water sources.

**Cairn Reach Reservoir** is an important reserve within a wider source portfolio.
Merewick Bulk Water Cooperative supplies neighbouring systems from a separate,
larger source and storage portfolio. A longstanding regional agreement permits
ARWC to purchase limited bulk deliveries through an existing utility connection.
Availability depends on Merewick's own commitments and conditions. Its different
source exposure explains why it can sometimes sell capacity during a locally dry
period; its capacity is neither unlimited nor guaranteed in every regional drought.

Most of the utility's work is smaller than a major supply decision: replacing
aging meters, renewing short lengths of pipe, maintaining treatment equipment,
running laboratory work, responding to account queries, and coordinating street
work with the districts. The company has enough routine successes that people
can be proud of doing their jobs well without a special occasion.

The merger is one layer of history. Many employees joined a company that was
already regional, and identify more with their shift, profession, or hiring
cohort than with either predecessor. Pine and River are places where people
live as well as administrative labels.

Most operational and customer-service positions are covered by established
collective agreements; management and some professional positions have separate
employment arrangements. Training, roster planning, progression, and staff
representation are normal working subjects. People can have different views
about a proposal without a labour dispute being the company's defining story.

Employees stay for useful local work, established benefits, colleagues they
trust, and opportunities to develop a profession without moving away. Some leave
for larger employers or different specialisms. Apprentices rotate through planned
learning assignments under supervision. Experienced employees can pursue
training, technical work, or supervisory posts; expertise does not require
everyone to want a manager's job.

### Culture in practice

ARWC's formal voice is measured because its decisions have owners and a public
audience. Its private working voice varies substantially. A familiar depot crew
can settle lunch arrangements in half a conversation. Customer-service staff
improvise explanations all day. Laboratory colleagues care about method but
also remember who helped them prepare for an assessment. Engineering discussions
can be lively even when the final record is restrained.

A new operator gains standing by learning the work, asking sensible questions,
and remembering what a colleague showed them. A new planner gains standing by
visiting the people behind a schedule. Neither has to perform instant confidence.
Mina is particularly attentive to someone asking a second question after receiving
an answer they did not quite understand.

Useful recognition often travels sideways: a customer-service colleague passes
along a resident's thanks; a crew mentions that a clearer notice made a visit
easier; someone credits the apprentice who prepared an excellent explanation.
Luc likes formal recognition more than some recipients do. He is learning to
ask whether someone actually wants to speak at a staff event.

There are familiar irritations. Daytime office events can be awkward for shift
staff. A document can be perfectly clear to its authors and bewildering to a new
customer. Colleagues sometimes prefer an old meeting place because that is where
they know the people, rather than because it is convenient. These differences
produce adjustment and occasional grumbling alongside a functioning service.

### What it wants next

ARWC's next five-year programme combines steady asset renewal with workforce
succession and service for modest regional growth. Public discussion concerns
the pace and cost of planned improvements. Staff want enough training capacity
to pass on expertise without asking the same experienced people to teach every
new intake while doing their full ordinary jobs.

The immediate calendar includes autumn training, routine pipe and meter work,
customer information sessions, the annual staff walk, and preparation for winter.
The dry-period allocation review is one current business responsibility among
these. It does not define every department's week or every employee's ambitions.
